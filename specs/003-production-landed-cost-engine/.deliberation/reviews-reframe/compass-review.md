# Compass review: Beans Control Center (reframe)

Strategy and EOS faculty. The scorecard, the handful of numbers a founder must see, and the discipline of not drowning a command center in vanity metrics.

## 1. The actual problem

The founder runs Beans across three countries from scattered WhatsApp threads. There is no single place that tells him whether the business is healthy right now. Costing was never the real problem. The real problem is that the state of the business lives in his head and in chat history, so he cannot see production, cash, stock, and margin at once, and he cannot tell what needs his attention today. The command center is a scorecard with a drill-down, not a reporting tool.

## 2. Smallest first version

Two status tiles plus two attention signals earn the home screen first.

Tiles: (a) Produccion esta semana (units produced or arrived against the weekly target), (b) Caja (cash on hand and simple runway in weeks). These are the two numbers a small physical-product founder checks daily.

Attention signals: (a) low coffee stock, (b) overdue supplier check-in. Both are concrete, both are actionable from the screen, both come from data the platform already holds or will hold early. Margin and landed cost are a tile later, not first, because the costing model is not populated on day one and a tile over empty data is noise.

## 3. Three risks

1. Wall of vanity metrics. The costing arc is rich and the temptation is to surface every input cost and every country delta on the home. A scorecard is leading indicators the founder acts on weekly, five to eight numbers, not fifty. Everything else is one drill-down away. Guard: a tile earns the home only if a bad reading changes what the founder does this week.
2. Attention strip that cries wolf. If the strip fires on soft thresholds it gets ignored inside two weeks, and then it is dead. Every signal needs a concrete trigger, a clear owner, and a way to clear it. A signal with no action behind it does not belong on the strip.
3. Modules that show numbers the founder cannot act on from the screen. A customs line he cannot fill, a cost he cannot change, is a report, not a command. Each module tile must link to the one action that moves it.

## 4. Success at 90 days

Measured in decisions made from the screen, not in features shipped. Success is: the founder opens the control center first each morning instead of scrolling WhatsApp; the attention strip drives at least one concrete action per week (reorder coffee, chase a check-in, fill a pending customs line); and he sets or revises a sale price from the costing module at least once, grounded in a landed cost he trusts. If he still runs the business from chat, the shell failed regardless of polish.

## 5. Atomic tasks

Headline scorecard (4 to 6): units produced this week vs target; cash on hand and runway in weeks; coffee stock weeks remaining; landed cost per bracelet (one lead type, one country) once populated; gross margin at current sale price; open attention count.

Attention-signal rules first: low stock (any key input below N weeks of coverage); pending cost line (a landed cost rendering with a PENDING component); overdue supplier check-in (no reply from Maria or Victor in X days); cash runway (runway below N weeks).

Module order: Home/Panel, Proveedores plus check-ins, Costos y margen, Inventario/Stock and Caja, Produccion, Logistica, Asistente.

## 6. What only my faculty noticed

The scattered WhatsApp business already has a rhythm, it just is not written down. The founder messages Maria and Victor on a loose cadence and reacts when something slips. The command center should not impose a new EOS rhythm on him. It should make his existing rhythm visible: the check-in cadence becomes the overdue-check-in signal, his reorder habit becomes the low-stock signal. Codify the rhythm he already runs; do not invent one he will abandon.
